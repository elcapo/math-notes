iov_ftc_01_exists_vs_closedform	The construction form gives: <b>every</b> continuous f has an antiderivative — yet ∫e^(−x²)dx has <b>no</b> antiderivative in closed form. Why is this not a contradiction?	Two different claims are in play. <b>Existence</b>: G(x) = ∫ₐˣ f(t)dt is well-defined (a definite signed area for each x), and the construction form proves G′ = f, so the antiderivative exists. <b>Closed form</b>: whether that G can be <b>rewritten</b> as a finite combination of elementary functions. The first is guaranteed; the second can fail. "Has an antiderivative" ≠ "the antiderivative is writable in elementary terms".	fundamental-theorem antiderivative closed-form existence	Basic	Maths::Integration of One Variable::Fundamental Theorem
iov_ftc_02_approximation_not_the_test	Tempting claim: "erf(1) = ∫₀¹ e^(−t²)dt has no closed form because we can only approximate its value." Use √2 to show the reasoning is broken.	√2 can <b>also</b> only be approximated as a decimal (1.4142…), yet "√2" <b>is</b> a closed-form expression. So "only approximable" holds for √2 and erf(1) alike — it fails to separate them, hence it cannot be what "closed form" means. Closed form is a property of the <b>symbolic expression</b> (a finite combination of admitted symbols and operations), not of the decimal expansion of the value.	fundamental-theorem closed-form misconception	Basic	Maths::Integration of One Variable::Fundamental Theorem
iov_ftc_03_relative_to_dictionary	In what sense is "has a closed form" <b>not</b> an absolute property of a function? What happens to ∫e^(−t²)dt once erf is admitted into the vocabulary?	Closed form is relative to a <b>chosen dictionary</b> of admitted functions (polynomials, exp, log, trig, roots…). Add erf to that dictionary — exactly as sin and log were once named and tabulated — and ∫₀ˣ e^(−t²)dt = (√π/2)·erf(x) <b>is</b> closed form in the enlarged vocabulary. "Elementary" is a convention about the toolkit, not an intrinsic trait of the function.	fundamental-theorem closed-form convention	Basic	Maths::Integration of One Variable::Fundamental Theorem
iov_ftc_04_nonelementary_is_provable	"∫e^(−x²)dx is not elementary" — is this merely "nobody has found the formula yet", or something stronger? Which classical fact about √2 is the right analogy?	Something stronger: <b>Liouville's theorem</b> proves that <b>no</b> finite combination of elementary functions has derivative e^(−x²). It is a proven impossibility <b>within a fixed dictionary</b>, not a gap in cleverness. The right analogy is "√2 is irrational" — also a proof of non-existence within a fixed system (the rationals), not a number we have failed to write as a fraction. So erf is to the elementary functions what √2 is to the rationals.	fundamental-theorem closed-form liouville	Basic	Maths::Integration of One Variable::Fundamental Theorem
